# Deepfakes - The Danger Of Artificial Intelligence That We Will Learn To Manage Better

*[Image: Deep Fakes are here to stay — Lutz Finger]*

Deepfakes are scarily simple to create. But will this technology create a reality of "alternative facts" where "truth goes to die"? No. Deepfakes are a technology, and more widespread abuse is expected with more widespread availability. Over time, we will adopt better transparency, better detection, and, most importantly, each of us users will become more aware and thus better equipped to fight the abuse of Deepfakes.

What are deepfakes? Deepfakes are a way to manipulate images. Image manipulation is not new — Stalin removed Nikolai Yezhov from still images. Today, the underlying technology is Artificial Intelligence. Check out the Tiktok compilation about Tom Cruise — FAKE. Or the video of Barack Obama calling Trump names — also FAKE. Today, anyone can create a deepfake. No programming skills are needed. Deepfakes are widely used today — in Hollywood for de-aging in Marvel movies or the late Paul Walker in Fast & Furious 7. Deepfakes have the potential to replace high-end CGI, saving millions of dollars and countless hours of processing time in filmmaking. But deepfakes can be — and are — abused. When email made it possible to communicate with large numbers of strangers for free, it immediately led to spam. When computers could communicate through networks, it spawned viruses and Trojan horses. Now we're dealing with deepfakes. The way to deal with this threat remains the same: detection, transparency, regulation, and education.

1. Detection. A growing number of researchers are studying fake news and building technologies to identify deepfakes. Big Tech firms like Google, Microsoft, and Meta have openly condemned deepfake technology and are creating tools for recognizing them. Microsoft is creating anti-deep-fake technology (Microsoft Video Authenticator). Meta's deepfake detector challenge has encouraged researchers and developers to create algorithms to fight deepfakes. Still, the best deepfake detector is only 65% accurate. Even Azure Cognitive Services was fooled over 78% of the time. The future of these detectors will mirror bot detectors, spam detectors, or any other cyber threat detectors — it will be an arms race.

2. Transparency. Social networks have allowed us to connect with everyone. This new connectedness means every fringe opinion will find its audience. Lies and fake news have become a business model. Social media companies created "verified accounts" to instill trust, but in a world of deepfake abundance, verified accounts alone will not be sufficient. One proposition to combat deepfakes points to the usage of NFTs — if everyone encrypted their videos as NFTs, it would be easy to find and compare different sources of the same moment.

3. Regulation. Misuse of deepfakes without clear identification should be outlawed — regulators around the globe have started to take them seriously. Already in 2019 there were about a dozen federal- and state-level bills to regulate deepfakes, ranging from criminalizing the use of a woman's likeness in a pornographic film without her consent to dealing with cheap fakes.

4. Education. Coming back to the Nigerian Prince scams. Today, many won't fall for them anymore — because you've all heard about it. Education is the best protection. And just like with the Nigerian Prince, we need the same degree of education for deepfakes. Channel 4 in the UK created a deepfake of the Queen's speech last Christmas — hilarious and bringing deepfakes into the public discussion. For my course at Cornell, where I teach MBA candidates to design products that use Data and AI, we start each term with a 1-minute video summary that we created to welcome the students. Yes, it's fun. But it's also a reminder that great technology can be abused.
