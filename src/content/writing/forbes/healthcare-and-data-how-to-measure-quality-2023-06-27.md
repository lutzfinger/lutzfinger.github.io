# Healthcare And Data - How To Measure Quality

*[Image: Health Care Quality Measures: Which data should you trust?]*

We rate Uber drivers, restaurants, Amazon delivery, but when it comes to our own health experience, we have little to no data. The business model of the healthcare provider is to get money from the insurance company — the patient is not the customer. Knowing quality is therefore of utmost importance. Three broad measurement areas in today's healthcare: (1) Metrics about quality of care — related to treatment effectiveness and patient satisfaction. (2) Metrics about need and utilization of care — gauging health requirements of a population and how often one uses what type of service. (3) Metrics about value-based healthcare — they measure disease prevention rather than disease treatment, measuring actions taken to prevent or reverse illnesses.

QUALITY METRIC. Quality of care metrics provide a way to measure and control the effectiveness, safety, and efficiency of care provided. Organizations like the National Quality Forum (NQF), Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ), and Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) each follow unique sets of metrics: Hospital Readmission Rates (gauges whether a patient stays out of the hospital after discharge), Mortality Rates (ensures patient survival), Patient Experience (assesses satisfaction levels), and Patient Safety Indicators (track in-hospital complications). As Michael Howell reminded me: "We have many metrics, but the real challenges are in finding the metrics that matter and preventing unintended consequences with any given metric." Take a simple metric on mortality for surgery — patients should not die during surgery. But what if the patient's situation was complex to start with? There are cases where a patient did not receive care and died because giving him appropriate care could have resulted in a worse quality score for the doctor.

NEED & UTILIZATION METRICS. These metrics are associated with population health costs of care. Need metrics look at population health measures such as the prevalence of diseases, morbidity, and health risk factors. Utilization metrics look at whether someone should have gotten a given intervention. As Marty Makary told me: "It is not sufficient to just measure the quality of medical procedures, but we also need to evaluate the appropriateness of these interventions." The current fee-for-service payment structure might encourage some doctors to perform multiple surgeries rather than one, as they are paid per procedure. Transparency in correcting misalignments is key.

VALUE-BASED CARE METRICS. Value-based care aims to put the patient in the center — a doctor or healthcare provider will only get paid when they improve your health. Measuring health improvement is difficult: is it cost? life expectancy? quality life years? A mix? At Marpai Connect we use: Bio Markers that are clinically proven (e.g., HBA1C measurements for diabetes), Other Measured Outcomes (Pain Reduction scores, functional health improvement scores), and Cost (easy for a third-party administrator to measure via claims, but cost alone is not sufficient). What we de-emphasize: Engagement metrics, because they often lack evidence that they work. Our approach is working. 40% of the commercial market wants to get access to value-based care. Let's help to make it accessible to everyone.
