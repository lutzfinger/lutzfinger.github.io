# How COVID Changed Our Classrooms? A Self-Experiment Of Online Teaching.

*[Image: Online teaching experiment during COVID-19]*

The COVID-19 crisis has changed many things. My course for Cornell's MBA and computer sciences students is now virtual. The challenge ahead is not about how to use Zoom's features, but about reinventing the whole course experience. In the last few weeks, I changed much of my course content, tried to make the best out of asynchronous vs. synchronous activities, and analyzed data to offer a great class during the shelter in place.

In 2012, a dean of a worldwide renowned business school told me: "MOOCs are going to be big." At that time, companies like Coursera and edX were going to change the world of education. This revolution has not happened, at least not yet. Universities have high fixed costs. Online so far offered only minimal marginal revenue. A traditional classroom was more adaptive than MOOCs — teachers adapt their speed to students, and students bring diverse previous experiences that are as important as the actual lecture itself.

Learning from COVID online teaching — three key lessons:

(1) Flip the classroom. The flipped classroom is not a new concept. The premise: ask students to read the content before class, then focus class time on applied exercises and discussions. Content curation is your critical path. I asked students to do a quiz before coming to class — this way I found out which areas students did not understand. For example, a video from FiveThirtyEight explains how k-means is used to cluster Margaritas. It's fun, but from the quiz I can tell it fails in explaining where the 'k' in k-means came from. I spent more time on this point in class than I would have usually done.

(2) Ensure engagement. The concept of "student engagement" is predicated on the belief that learning improves when students are interested. I experimented with cold-calling via a random number generator, multiple-choice questionnaires, discussions in smaller groups, and other methods. I surveyed students after each class — multiple-choice questionnaires came out on top. I created multiple-choice questionnaires for every 10 minutes of my course. They ensured my speed was right, and measured my teaching performance. I also used a Google Spreadsheet — posed a discussion topic and asked students to outline their answer in one to two sentences. I can follow live and pick the most interesting answers to discuss in class. Pre-screening of arguments works extremely well to create robust class discussion outcomes. Doing this class online has created a more inclusive classroom setting — traditional classrooms favored extroverts, but pre-screening answers and quizzes let everyone participate.

(3) Be approachable. A personal relationship between a prof and a student is a key driver for learning. How do you create a bond when you're a small sub-window on top of a Google presentation? I went off presentation mode as often as possible so that everyone is more visible. I spiced up the course with fun actions using Snap Cam to change myself into a potato or come in a Tuxedo to class.

COVID has changed our classrooms. Going from a traditional classroom to online distance learning doesn't happen overnight. Digital tools and data started to help me create an engaging learning environment. Learning won't be the same — it will be better.
