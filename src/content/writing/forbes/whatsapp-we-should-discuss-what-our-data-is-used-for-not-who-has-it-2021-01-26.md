# WhatsApp: We Should Discuss What Our Data Is Used For, Not Who Has It

*[Image: We should discuss what our data is used for, not who has it]*

If you are on WhatsApp, you will have realized an outcry over the updated privacy policy where they disclose what data they will share with their mother company Facebook. The changes are not dramatic and did not even matter for Europeans. But it does not matter what Facebook does — it matters that we don't trust Facebook. Why? Because we don't know what happens with our data. We should not discuss who has access to the data but, more importantly, what is the data used for?

Data can be helpful. From Waze to Netflix, recommendation systems are great. We want those apps to use our data and give us the service we love. In healthcare, we want doctors to use health data to diagnose our potential issues. But we do not expect our doctor to share health information with an insurance company that will then change the pricing of our life insurance. The healthcare industry is regulated. But the social media industry is not. Already in 2015, I was approached by a large European insurance company who wanted to know whether they could use deep learning to identify riskier sports, like skydiving in social media photos.

Data should be used to make our lives better and more productive. But if data is used without our knowledge to assess whether you will commit a crime, whether you are fit for college, or whether you should get a loan, then we will — rightly — feel uncomfortable. Michal Kosinski already showed in 2013 that using Facebook data could accurately predict sexual orientation, ethnicity, personality traits, intelligence, happiness, addictive substances use, parental separation, age, and gender. The model even managed to separate between Democrat and Republican in 85% of cases. We don't trust Facebook anymore because we learned that they gave away our data way too carelessly.

WhatsApp is probably not the biggest privacy offender we have in our phones. Phone companies Sprint, AT&T, T-Mobile, and Verizon sold our location data. There is an entire ecosystem of apps that share a lot of information about us. During the pandemic, many companies created dashboards showing aggregated movement data — revealing that smaller companies like Unacast and Placer.ai gathered location information via hundreds of apps on your phone. The question should always be: for what do you need this information?

We need a better framework to work with data. GDPR demands that organizations only collect personal data for a specific purpose, clearly stating it. Companies often do not know the value of the data they collect — Facebook did not know what data would drive their business model's value for quite a while. Regulation of data use will force companies to ask for consent before developing new algorithms. This extra step will inadvertently slow down innovation — and might be a factor in the global innovation competition. China can correlate and analyze datasets without regulatory constraints, creating a competitive advantage. We need to regulate our markets and expect international partners to comply with the same rules. We should push for clear regulations and international norms on transparency, accountability, and data ownership. Only then will we start to trust again.
