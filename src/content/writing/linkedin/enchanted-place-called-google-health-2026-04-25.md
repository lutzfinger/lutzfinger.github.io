Enchanted Place Called Google Health
Google is truly a fantastic place. No, I am not talking about the colorful bikes, the great food, the free perks, and more. After four years of building what Google Health is today, here is my view of what makes Google so unique: people and processes.
After I helped Snap go public, I wanted to do more with data and AI than improve kids' streaks using Snap. I wanted to explore the use of data and AI to improve healthcare, something that I had been dreaming about already since 2014 (interview with O'Reilly) after publishing my book on big data. It turns out that also Google thought a lot about healthcare, and for a good reason: 7% of their searches are healthcare-related (Thanks @Nisha). Therefore I joined their then-nascent healthcare effort in Google Brain and helped over the last four years to build out Google Health. It was an inspiring ride! We did fantastic research (thanks @Alvin); we launched Googley products (thanks @Eyal); we got challenged by the press (or were these healthcare incumbents?); we helped the world with COVID forecast (thanks @Kathryn & @Helen) and mobility data (thanks @Evgeniy); we improved healthcare information on search (thanks @Peter & @Yanif); we had big plans (thanks @Salim); we hired David; we lost David; we got customers (thanks @Sri and @Elise); we got research partners (thanks @Doris); we charted (thanks @Claudia & @Cristian); we built more (thanks @Siqi, @Ethan, @Yi, and @Ming-Jun); and we envisioned even more... Every day on the fast track. I was fortunate to take part in this journey (thanks, @Paul and @Tom), and I am humbled to help set up and grow our population health effort. It was a magical time in an enchanted place.
Beyond the specifics of working as a Product Manager at Google (TL;DR: meetings, meetings, alignment, OKRs, occasional conflicts and did I mention meetings?), two areas stand out which make Google special: People and Processes.
PEOPLE
Anyone who joins Google will quickly get a Google-specific variant of an "impostor syndrome." The company is full of geniuses. There are three types of "genius" at Google: the wizards, the magnets, and the visionaries…
Let's start with the wizards. I vividly recall the first time I met @Nissan - who is now in his 18th year at Google. We were in one of those barista places sipping a handcrafted coffee. It took only a short question on measuring model quality, and Nissan would take to the closest whiteboard and sketch out his thoughts and explain the effectiveness of different measurements. A geek? Far from it. A while later, he would talk about his climbing passion. Nissan is one of those wizard Googlers: He knows everything about; in his case, Machine Learning but also knows something about everything. Google is an engineering-focused culture, and folks like Nissan will enable product ideas and visions of us Product Managers.
The other group is the magnets. They attract and keep talent. Google promotes a high level of employee mobility. If you are unhappy with your current team, you have a lot of interesting areas where you could move to within Google. And more often than not, people will follow magnets. Magnets are not just managers; they deeply care, they want you to succeed, they guide and support you. One of them is @Hein, a tall engineering director. Discussions with him always were somewhere between inspirational and mind-challenging. If you are lost, he magically was there to help you. His caring attitude shows. Where Hein is, there is talent. In my initial time at Google, I looked at the consumer health strategy with Hein. And very soon, a team of wizards started to come together, folks like @Fred, @James, @Victor, @Yanif, and @Yohann. Nothing seemed impossible, but everything was very grounded in engineering reality. Magnets make Google the place today, caring, loving, fun.
Lastly, there are the visionaries. Many folks at Google want to "do the right thing" (the adjusted version from 'don't be evil') and will create an amazing pool of product ideas. For tech giants, there are many tempting ideas in healthcare. Healthcare uses - let's say - "outdated" tech. Faxes are still the norm, and tech sellers try to up-talk 'heuristic rules' as world-breaking AI. But where do we start? We are Google; it should be big, it should be helpful, it should be within our abilities. Those are the callings for the visionaries Google. Visionaries like @Michael Howell - a former associate professor at Harvard Medical School and the former Chief Quality Officer at the University of Chicago Medicine.
I am thankful for all his work and support to take apart the different parts of the vast and complex ecosystem of health. I don't know how many charters and strategy documents I have created during my time at Google. Let's say "a lot." To separate a pipe-dream from reality, we needed a 'north star,' which Michael brought us back to many times. I vividly recall one strategy meeting in the formative days of Google Health where one of my team members got up to pitch, "Let's do Animal health instead of healthcare." The market might be big, but it's entirely not what we wanted to do.
My team set out to build Pop Health predictions using data and AI. AI can predict illnesses and preventable situations, but who can act on that information? (thanks @Laura and @Meredith) How do I incentivize the right parts of the healthcare system? (thanks Stuart) And the list of "How do I" questions goes on. Thanks to @Von, @Birju, and all the other visionaries for keeping us on track.
PROCESSES
Do you recall the 2009 Appstore Commercial "There is an App for that"? The gist of the commercial is that whatever you need to do with the new iPhone, there will be an app for it. Google could make a similar commercial for processes. "There is a process for that." Whether it's hiring decisions, OKRs, promotions, or new product launches - there is a process for that," or at least a form and maybe even a committee. And a group of program managers keeping everyone sane (thanks @Jenni, @Victor, @Sveta, @Mitch and @I-Ching). Sounds scary? Google is not like the company version of the 1985 film Brazil where form 27B-6 let folks go insane. No, at Google, everyone complains about the processes but also everyone constantly fine-tunes them to put excellence into process format. Google is run on the idea that humans can fail, and we need to build processes to help reduce failure as much as possible. This feedback loop creates those high levels of excellence within Google.
Let's take Google's OKR process. It's long, overly engineered, and it is en-vogue to complain over a coffee about it. But it helps to drive organizational alignment and reduces ambiguity. One of my tasks at Google was to align part of our research efforts with products. I worked here closely with the former research team from DeepMind. The INFORMATION published a pretty negative picture of the organizational mood. We went through weeks of alignment and vision exercises (thanks to @Alan, @Martin). As painful and cumbersome as it was - Google's processes helped us drive towards transparency, understanding, and alignment.
While powerful in keeping Google excellency, processes are often quoted as slowing innovation. The solution? You guessed it, "more processes." The Google answer to this innovator dilemma is 20% projects. Essentially if you have an idea and sufficient support from your engineers, try it. This kind of Grassroot movement helped create Chrome, Gmail, and other Google products. VPs at Google act as a kind of Venture Capitalist, deciding what gets funded and what does not. Indeed, not every idea creates a unicorn, as you can see in https://killedbygoogle.com/, but the vast amount of 'dead-ends' shows you the innovative power.
But here comes the catch. Healthcare does not support such a 'Googely' way of innovation. Often to conduct research or explore a new area, you need to get patient consent, you need an Institutional Review Boards (IRBs), and so on. All of this takes time; all of this is more complex. The good news is - working for Google it's not impossible. For example, to launch our first initial predictive models, the team and I wrote about 40 thousand (!) words outside of any internal processes to overcome and tame the very processes (thanks @Stefanie, @Lihong, @Shilpa, and @Mike Willingham). Painful? For sure. Often more complicated than it needs to be? Yes, absolutely? But feasible - that's what makes Google such an amazing place.
WHY DO I LEAVE?
Why do I leave, you ask? The last four years showed me the value of data and AI to healthcare. See my mini-pitch for any founder on why one should think more about technology and healthcare. But as one of my favorite bloggers, @Nikhil, described nicely in six stages of healthcare grief, it's not tech alone. To help everyone live more life every day, one needs to be close to everyone. Healthcare is a service industry and can use AI and data to augment and improve. Thus I am joining Marpai Health as president of product and technology to create an AI-driven approach to proactive healthcare. To enable Marpai's members to live better through better health.
